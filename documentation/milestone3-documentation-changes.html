1.2 Current Situation, needs, ideas

1.2.1 Current Situation

Eating at the university cafeteria is a necessity for many students, yet the current environment at the cafeteria is incapable of adequately meeting its high demand. The university cafeteria consistently experiences long lines and heavy traffic, particularly during peak hours. This inefficiency creates a recurring dilemma where students must choose between securing a meal or arriving on time for their next academic commitment. When students are forced to skip meals due to these waiting times, it can result in a substantial negative impact on their overall health, mental focus, and academic performance. The primary cause of this issue is an outdated manual, in-person ordering system that is simply not capable of handling a high volume of students in a short time frame. Currently in the cafeteria, every order and payment transaction is processed manually at the counter, leading to delays during peak demand periods. On a typical visit to the current cafeteria, a student walks to the cafeteria, checks the daily menu posted at the counter, and decides what to eat. To obtain the food, the student joins a single line that leads to the service counter. When the student reaches the counter, they state their order, the student pays for their order at the counter, cafeteria staff serve the food or start preparing it, and then the student picks up the meal when it is ready. Every student must pass through the same sequence of events: join the queue, order, pay, wait for preparation, and pick up the meal. The lack of a structured ordering system leads to long queues, increased waiting times, and operational inefficiencies that affect both students and cafeteria staff.

1.2.2 Needs

The long wait times at the university cafeteria show unmet needs in food service and campus life, not in any specific system or technology. These needs come from how student schedules, cafeteria operations, and institutional restrictions interact, and as a consequence, they affect many stakeholders.

Students

Access meals reliably within a limited time window without having to risk tardiness, academic responsabilities and basic needs

Cafeteria Staff

Be able to handle high order volumes efficiently at any time while mantaining a manageable and organized operational flow.

University Staff

Ensure that campus food services support the student’s wellness and success, doing so while remaining within any resource and staffing constraints

Ordering

All parties, both customers and staff, need the ordering process to be as clear, accurante and unambiguous. Customers need menu visibility and availability, and staff requires orders to be communicated without error.

Paying

Customers need clear information of the amount they are expected to pay, along with the order’s payment confirmation and staff need a reliable way to verify the customer’s payment before releasing the meal.

Preparing

The staff need to align preparation work based on average customer demand during normal or peak periods to reduce food waste and update customers with their order’s preparation status and time.

Pickup

The pickup process needs to be ordered and accurate. Customers need to be accurately notified when their order is ready and the staff needs a reliable way to match the correct order with the customer.

Project Level Needs

A baseline for the project’s requirements, common language format for any documentation belonging to the project, doccument planning and a general description of the Cafeteria Ordering operations to show the project from research through implementation.

1.2.3 Ideas

Based on the previously discussed needs, a platform where students and staff from the university community can easily order food from the university cafeteria can drastically reduce the waiting time and facilitate staff work. The Cafeteria ordering application can help the aforementioned needs with features such as:

  • Ordering: Now, it’s easier than ever to order from the cafeteria! When a user wants to order an item, the available menu will be displayed with different sections depending on the type of food, sides, snacks and beverages. If an item from the menu is not available, it will not be able to be selected to order. After the user picks out what they want to order, they can choose whether to pay at the register or pay on the app, then, simply click order and done!

  • Payment Options: The user can choose to pay for their order through the app, which will allow them to skip the line and pick up their order as soon as it’s ready. Alternatively, they can choose to pay at the register, which will allow them to pick up their order as soon as they pay for it. In both cases, the system creates an order record owned by the customer, but differ in how the order is processed. An in-app payment marks the order as "Payment Completed" immediately, so it can be prepared and picked up when ready. Payment at register creates a "Pending Payment" order that is tentative until paid. This kind of order must not be held indefinitely, so it can be canceled by the user and should time out if the user does not show up to pay. If after a set interval the user doesn’t pay, the order transitions to "Cancelled Order" to avoid preparing food that will not be picked up. If the customer attempts to pay after the payment window has already expired, cafeteria staff must verify whether the order is still available before accepting payment. Orders that have already been removed or cancelled must be reordered by the customer. As some orders are paid in the platform and others at the register, staff must be able to quickly verify payment status at pickup and reconcile register payments with the correct order record to avoid double payment, missed payments, or wrong order payments.

  • Pick up times: The user can choose whether to schedule to pick up an order, or also, order instantly to be pick up as soon as possible.

  • Notification system: The project will include notifications for both the user and the cafeteria staff to keep them informed about the status of an order, to improve communication and help coordinate order preparation and pickup.

  • Order security: when the user wants to pick up an order in the designated pick-up station, the user will be given a code that they have to confirm with the staff to then be able to pick up their order, vastly reducing the possibility of having a customer’s order stolen.

  • Cafeteria mode: For the staff to monitor the orders, an “admin mode” will be integrated so that all the orders along with their pickup time will be displayed for the cafeteria staff, as well as notify them for order cancellations, any notes from the orders, etc. The staff will be able to input when an order is being processed, and when the order has been completed.

  • Feedback: The app will ask the users for any feedback after an order has been processed and confirmed, this way, the team can make improvements and tweaks to the application and make the service better.

The application can help students mantain an easier routine for their everyday life, cafeteria staff can be more organized and also cuts down on miscommunication when people have orders taken in person.

1.3 Scope, Span and Synopsis

1.3.1 Scope and Span

Scope

While anyone at the university is welcome to purchase from the cafeteria, this application is primarily intended for students, who represent the cafeteria’s largest customer group and face the most significant challenges described in this document. The scope of the project is to develop a digital ordering and pickup platform designed to eliminate the current logistical bottlenecks in the university cafeteria. The platform addresses the inefficiencies of manual counter service that the UPRM cafeteria relies on that forces students to choose between nutrition and being on time for their academic responsibilities.

Domain Engineering: Analyze how the current university food service is offered and identify pain points experienced by students and staff with in-person ordering. This involves structuring the relationships among ordering, payment, preparation, and pickup so that the team develops a shared understanding of cafeteria workflows and operational constraints before moving toward a specific solution.

Requirements Engineering: Discover what prospective users need from the system and capture those findings as prescriptions for system behavior. This includes eliciting user expectations around viewing menus, managing a digital cart, and processing remote payments. It also includes engineering the logistics for a notification system, which alerts staff of new orders and notifies students upon order completion, and a secure order pickup protocol using one-time passcodes. These prescriptions must be made explicit from the perspective of students, staff, and administrators so that later design and implementation decisions are guided by stakeholder needs rather than assumptions.

Software Architecture: The architecture will consist of a web front-end for students and staff. It will integrate a backend ordering engine with a simulation environment in place of a physical infrastructure component: cafeteria workers, pickup areas, and order states will be simulated to enable end-to-end testing without requiring physical deployment. Because there is no existing digital infrastructure supporting peak-hour cafeteria operations, early architectural planning is necessary to define responsibilities clearly and reduce the risk of poorly structured design decisions.

Software Design Process: The project will follow an iterative, test-driven development lifecycle. A standardized process for handling order flows and issues will be defined, and a continuous feedback loop with cafeteria staff and students will be used to refine the user interface, pickup flow, and operational procedures over time. The process will also include early planning for testing and evaluation so that improvements such as reduced waiting times, smoother pickup, reliability, and usability can be assessed in a structured way.

Span

The project’s span is focused on creating a localized, high-efficiency solution for the university cafeteria that streamlines the food procurement process.

The application provides users with temporal flexibility, allowing for "ASAP" orders or specific scheduled pickup times. It also includes a dedicated pickup zone that requires a unique code for access, addressing the risk of order theft.

The target audience are UPRM students and staff at the university cafeteria. However, the project also seeks the possibility to include other campus food vendors and integration with university events, such as Come Colegial.

The project will utilize agile development, following its iterative steps, with primary focus on testing, to achieve project completion. In addition, the team will apply a Test-Driven Development approach named red, green, and refactor. Through this approach, developers will write failing tests for small, requirement-derived behaviors before implementing the minimum code needed to pass them, and then refactor the code to improve code quality and maintainability.

Success will be tracked through specific metrics: the volume of orders processed, the speed of order pickup, and the measurable reduction in counter wait times. Additionally, the system will provide data analytics to help cafeteria administrators optimize inventory and staffing levels based on peak demand patterns.

1.3.2 Synopsis

The Cafeteria Ordering System project addresses operational inefficiencies observed in the university cafeteria, particularly during peak service hours. Long waiting times, limited menu visibility, and uncertainty in demand create negative impacts on student well-being and cafeteria workflow. Rather than approaching this situation as a purely technical problem, the project follows a structured software engineering process grounded in domain understanding and systematic analysis.

The project begins with a detailed domain description that formalizes observed phenomena within the cafeteria environment. Through domain sketches, terminology definition, and concept analysis, the team establishes a shared vocabulary that reflects real-world entities, actions, events, and behaviors. This foundation ensures clarity and consistency in subsequent requirements engineering activities.

Building upon the domain model, stakeholder needs are translated into epics, user stories, and structured requirements. These include domain requirements, interface requirements, and machine requirements with measurable performance, availability, and capacity criteria. By defining explicit constraints and measurable targets, the project ensures that any proposed solution aligns with time-sensitive cafeteria operations and institutional limitations.

Early architectural reasoning is incorporated to anticipate system structure and interactions between core components such as ordering, payment processing, notification handling, and pickup coordination. Testing strategies, including Test-Driven Development (TDD) and Behavior-Driven Development (BDD), are integrated into the planned workflow to maintain traceability between requirements and implementation while enabling structured validation and verification.

Throughout the project, documentation, milestone planning, coordinated team roles, ongoing stakeholder communication, progress tracking, and preparation for future iterations or expansion support alignment across analysis, design, testing, and implementation activities. By combining domain modeling, structured requirements engineering, architectural planning, systematic testing preparation, and strong project coordination, the project establishes a comprehensive and well-founded approach to improving cafeteria service efficiency and reliability.

1.4 Derived Goals

In addition to the primary goal of reducing wait times and improving access to meals in the university cafeteria, the project also pursues several secondary but meaningful outcomes.

By introducing a structured, mobile-based ordering approach, the system may serve as a model for how other campus services might modernize their processes. This broader institutional impact extends beyond the cafeteria itself and contributes to a more technology-integrated campus environment.

A further derived goal is to strengthen collaboration and coordination among stakeholders. The structured interaction between students, cafeteria staff, and university administration fosters clearer communication channels and more transparent service processes. Finally, the project seeks to raise awareness about the importance of aligning campus services with student needs. 2.1.2 Terminology

The terminology defined in this section is derived from recurring phenomena observed in the domain rough sketch. These terms are not arbitrary labels; rather, they represent abstractions of stakeholder experiences, operational constraints, and observable interactions within the cafeteria environment. Together, they establish a shared vocabulary for subsequent concept analysis and requirements work.

Customer

(entity, domain) A person who obtains food or beverages from the cafeteria, including students, faculty, staff, or visitors.

Student

(entity, domain) A person enrolled at the university who may act as a customer when obtaining food or beverages from the cafeteria, typically within limited time windows between academic activities or responsibilities.

Cafeteria

(entity, domain) A food-service environment on campus where meals are prepared, ordered, paid for, and collected.

Cafeteria Staff

(entity, domain) Staff responsible for preparing meals, handling orders, processing payments, and managing the cafeteria.

Meal

(entity, domain) A set of food and/or beverage items offered by the cafeteria during operating hours, including prepared items (e.g., hot dishes) as well as non-prepared items (e.g., bottled drinks, water, or packaged goods).

Menu

(entity, domain) A collection of meals and side options available during a specific period, whose visibility and accessibility may affect student decision-making before ordering.

Order

(entity, domain) A request made by a customer for one or more items from the menu.

Pickup Code

(entity, domain) A short numeric code generated at the time an order is placed, assigned to that order, and presented to the customer. The code is used at the pickup counter to verify that the correct customer is collecting the order, reducing the risk of incorrect handoff or order theft.

Queue

(entity, domain) An ordered sequence of students waiting to place an order or complete payment, especially visible during peak periods when lines extend beyond the primary service area.

Service Period

(entity, domain) A continuous time window during which the cafeteria is open and serving meals.

Order Placement

(action, domain) The act of selecting menu items and communicating the request to cafeteria staff.

Payment

(action, domain) The process of transferring money from a customer to the cafeteria in exchange for selected items, including the possibility of service disruption when payment processing fails. In the current domain, payment may occur at or after receiving the meal (e.g., upon tray pickup), rather than strictly at order placement. This behavior may change once the system is introduced.

Meal Preparation

(action, domain) The production of meals in advance or in response to incoming orders, often under uncertainty about near-term customer demand.

Meal Pickup

(action, domain) A customer receiving prepared or selected items after an order has been completed.

Waiting Time

(attribute, domain) The elapsed time between a customer joining the queue and receiving their meal, often influencing whether the customer remains in line or leaves due to time pressure.

Student Has Just Joined the Queue

(event, domain) The moment a student enters the line to place an order.

Order Has Just Been Placed

(event, domain) The moment a customer’s order is communicated to cafeteria staff.

Payment Has Just Failed

(event, domain) The moment a payment attempt does not complete successfully, causing a delay in service.

Meal Has Just Been Prepared

(event, domain) The moment a meal becomes ready for pickup.

Student Has Just Left the Queue Without Ordering

(event, domain) The moment a student abandons the line due to time pressure or other circumstances.

Crowd Surge

(behavior, domain) A situation that occurs when large numbers of students arrive within a short time spans. This leads to long queues and increased service pressure.

Time-Constrained Decision Making

(behavior, domain) A behavior in which customers evaluate whether to wait for food based on current queue conditions, expected waiting time, and proximity to their next responsibility.

Uncertain Demand Preparation

(behavior, domain) A pattern where cafeteria staff prepare meals without precise knowledge of near-term demand volume, creating pressure in periods of sudden customer arrival.

Service Bottleneck

(behavior, domain) A recurring slowdown caused by limited capacity in ordering, payment, or preparation, often becoming most visible during demand surges or operational failures.

The domain description above also establishes several important properties of the cafeteria environment that motivate the domain requirements. In this domain, every order contains at least one menu item and is associated with a specific customer. Menu items may become unavailable during a service period due to inventory limitations or preparation constraints. Orders progress through distinct preparation stages before they are eligible for pickup. Each menu item has an established price that contributes to the total cost of the order, and an order may only be released once preparation has been completed. The domain also includes the possibility of order cancellation before fulfillment, payment obligations that must be satisfied before release, customers who are not limited to students, and preparation times that may vary according to the order and current kitchen conditions.

2.1.3 Narrative

The university’s cafeteria functions as one of the primary food services that support the daily nutritional needs of students, staff, and faculty. Often individuals depend on the cafeteria to receive their prepared meals under tight schedules caused by multiple classes, team meetings, work responsibilities, or campus activities. Due to students having similar break periods, demand for the cafeteria’s services concentrates significantly around certain peak periods, most notably midday or the universal hour on Tuesdays and Thursdays.

The service process begins when a customer accesses the cafeteria and reviews available food options through the cafeteria’s menu. After reviewing the available options, the customer decides which meals they wish to receive.

To receive their meals, customers enter a service queue and await their turn at the service point. When a customer reaches the front of the queue, an order is placed by communicating the requested items to cafeteria staff, who are then responsible for fulfilling those requests. Some items may be immediately available, while others require preparation time, causing service duration to vary between orders.

Once the prepared items are presented to the customer, the customer proceeds to complete payment through the established cafeteria payment processes at the cashier station. Payment finalizes the transaction, and the customer exits the service, allowing the next individual in the queue to be served.

The arrival rate during peak demand periods frequently exceeds the service capacity of the cafeteria staff, lenghtening queues and increasing waiting times. Customers must often make time sensitive decisions whether to remain in line based on their upcoming academic or work obligations. From the other side, the cafeteria personnel experience increased workload, which can affect the preparation pace, communication, and overall service.

From an observational standpoint, this domain includes entities such as customers, cafeteria staff, menu items, orders, payments, preparation activities, and pickup events. Natural relationships exist among these entities: a customer selects one or more menu items, and an order aggregates the selected items associated with that customer. Cafeteria staff prepare the items associated with the order, and upon receiving the prepared items, the customer completes payment to finalize the transaction. The availability of menu items may change over time based on inventory levels and preparation constraints.

Environmental factors such as time of day, academic calendar events, present staff, and the physical layout of the cafeteria further influence the service performance and waiting times for the customer. These real world characteristics shape how the cafeteria operations unfold without the support of any software system.

2.1.4 Events, Actions, and Behaviors

This section catalogs the events, actions, and behaviors present in the cafeteria domain, grouped alongside their related domain entities rather than organized by type alone. The distinction is important because it clarifies what is observed as an instantaneous moment (event), what is performed as an act by a human or non-human actor (action), and what emerges as a process or recurring pattern over time (behavior).

There is a difference between:

  • the moment from which a student has just become a member of the queue (event),

  • the act of becoming a member of the queue (action),

  • and the process consisting of various smaller actions that together make up "becoming a member" during a service period (behavior).

Actions may be performed by human actors (students or cafeteria staff) or by non-human actors such as equipment or automated processes.

Queue and Customer Arrival

  • Joining a Queue (action) — the act of a student entering the service line to await their turn.

  • Student Has Just Joined the Queue (event) — the moment a student enters the line to place an order.

  • Student Has Just Left the Queue Without Ordering (event) — the moment a student abandons the line due to time pressure or other circumstances.

  • Crowd Surge (behavior) — Multiple students Join a queue in a short span of time → Student Has Just Joined the Queue is observed repeatedly → results in a more lengthy queue and increased service pressure on staff

  • Time-Constrained Decision Making (behavior) — Student Joins a queue → student contemplates about staying or leaving the queue line → if student left: Student Has Just Left the Queue Without Ordering

Order

  • Order Placement (action) — the act of selecting menu items and communicating the request to cafeteria staff.

  • Order Has Just Been Placed (event) — the moment a customer’s order is communicated to cafeteria staff.

Payment

  • Payment (action) — the process of transferring money from a customer to the cafeteria in exchange for selected items, including the possibility of disruption when payment processing fails.

  • Payment Has Just Failed (event) — the moment a payment attempt does not complete successfully, causing a delay in service.

  • Service Bottleneck (behavior) — Customer performs payment → staff troubleshoots, stalling queue → other customers try to join the queue → throughput is reduced → Student left queue without Ordering

Meal Preparation and Pickup

  • Meal Preparation (action) — the production of meals in advance or in response to incoming orders, often under uncertainty about near-term demand.

  • Meal Has Just Been Prepared (event) — the moment a meal becomes ready for pickup.

  • Meal Pickup (action) — a customer receiving prepared or selected items after an order has been completed.

  • Uncertain Demand Preparation (behavior) — Staff prepares meal without confirmed order → Meal is Prepared in advance → Food waste due to over-preparation or delays due to under-preparation depending on actual incoming orders

  • Browsing the Menu This operation relates to the Menu concept described in Sections 2.1.2 and 2.1.4. Retrieving the available food items requires access to the active menu catalog. viewMenu : MenuCatalog → List(MenuItem)

  • Managing the Order Selection This operation relates to the Order Placement action described in Section 2.1.4. When a customer adds an item, the operation alters the local state of their current order selection rather than the global database, minimizing dependencies. addItemToOrder : OrderSelection >< MenuItem >< Quantity → OrderSelection

  • Placing an Order This operation formalizes the Order Placement action and the Order Has Just Been Placed event described in Section 2.1.4. Placing an order requires the current order selection, the customer details, and the global order records. If items are out of stock or the order is invalid, the operation might fail, returning an Option for the generated order. placeOrder : OrderRecords >< OrderSelection >< Customer → OrderRecords >< (Option Order)

  • Processing Payments This operation relates to the Payment action and Payment Has Just Failed event described in Section 2.1.4. This operation takes the finalized order and a payment method, interacting with the payment system. It yields a receipt upon success or indicates a failure. processPayment : PaymentSystem >< Order >< PaymentMethod → PaymentSystem >< (Option Receipt)

  • Updating Order Status This operation relates to the Meal Preparation and Meal Pickup actions described in Section 2.1.4. As cafeteria staff prepare the food, they update the order’s progress (e.g., Pending, Preparing, Ready). This changes the state of the active order list. updateOrderStatus : ActiveOrderList >< OrderID >< Status → ActiveOrderList

  • Canceling an Order This operation relates to the order cancellation behavior discussed throughout the ordering and payment flow. If a customer or staff member cancels an order, the system must reverse the transaction, updating the records and potentially issuing a refund. cancelOrder : OrderRecords >< OrderID → OrderRecords >< (Option RefundStatus)

2.2 Requirements

2.2.1 User stories, epics, and features

This section organizes the system requirements from a high-level product perspective. Epics describe the major areas of value the system should provide, features describe the system capabilities needed to support those areas, and user stories describe how customers and cafeteria staff are expected to interact with the system. Together, these elements help connect the cafeteria problem context to specific system functionality.

Epics

Meal Ordering and Access

This epic focuses on improving how customers view, select, order, and receive meals during cafeteria service hours. The main goal is to reduce uncertainty for customers by giving them better visibility into available food and drink options before they commit to waiting in line or placing an order. This is especially important in a cafeteria environment where students or customers may have limited time between classes, work, or other responsibilities.

The system should support a smoother ordering experience by allowing customers to understand what is available, submit an order, receive order updates, and know when their meal is ready for pickup. This epic connects directly to features such as meal availability information, order placement, order status tracking, and pickup coordination.

Payment Processing

This epic focuses on supporting reliable and convenient payment handling as part of the cafeteria ordering process. Since customers may need to pay before or during pickup, the system must provide a clear payment flow that reduces confusion and prevents incomplete or incorrect orders from disrupting cafeteria operations.

The system should help customers understand the cost of their order, confirm payment status, and receive feedback if a payment cannot be completed. For cafeteria staff, this epic helps ensure that orders entering the preparation flow have the necessary payment information available. This epic connects to features such as order and payment handling, payment confirmation, and payment failure support.

Peak-Hour Flow Management

This epic focuses on helping cafeteria staff manage high customer demand during busy service periods. Peak hours can cause long lines, crowded waiting areas, delayed meal preparation, and confusion between customers and staff. The system should reduce these issues by improving visibility into incoming demand and supporting better coordination between ordering, preparation, and pickup.

The goal of this epic is not only to make the customer experience faster, but also to help staff make better operational decisions during stressful periods. This includes understanding how many orders are waiting, which meals are being requested, and where delays may occur in the service flow. This epic connects to features such as demand awareness support, order queue visibility, and service flow coordination.

Service Reliability and Coordination

This epic focuses on keeping cafeteria service organized and dependable from the moment an order is placed until the meal is picked up. The system should support coordination between customers and staff so that orders are not lost, delayed unnecessarily, or marked incorrectly.

A reliable service flow is important because cafeteria operations depend on multiple connected stages: customers selecting meals, the system recording orders, staff preparing items, and customers receiving pickup information. This epic connects to features such as order status tracking, staff order management, pickup notifications, and service disruption handling.

User Stories

As a Customer

  • I want to see available food and drink options before ordering, so that I can decide whether the cafeteria has something I want before spending time waiting.

  • I want to see whether menu items are currently available or unavailable, so that I do not place an order for an item that cannot be prepared.

  • I want to view the price and summary of my order before confirming it, so that I can verify that my order is correct.

  • I want to place an order through the system, so that I can reduce the amount of time I spend waiting in a physical line.

  • I want to know when my order is being prepared and when it is ready for pickup, so that I can better manage my time while waiting.

  • I want to receive a clear notification when my order is ready, so that I know when to go to the pickup area.

  • I want to be informed if my payment fails or my order cannot be completed, so that I can correct the issue without causing confusion for cafeteria staff.

As a Cafeteria Staff Member

  • I want to have visibility into incoming orders, so that I can prepare meals more efficiently and reduce delays during service hours.

  • I want to see the details of each order, including selected items and order status, so that I can prepare the correct meals for each customer.

  • I want to update the status of an order, so that customers know whether their order is pending, being prepared, or ready for pickup.

  • I want to mark menu items as unavailable when they run out, so that customers do not continue ordering items that cannot be served.

  • I want to manage service flow during busy periods, so that congestion, confusion, and long wait times are reduced.

  • I want to see demand information during peak hours, so that I can prioritize preparation and respond to high-volume periods more effectively.

  • I want the system to support coordination between ordering, preparation, and pickup, so that staff can maintain a more organized cafeteria workflow.

Features

Meal Availability Information

This feature provides customers with visibility into available meals and beverages during a cafeteria service period. It should allow customers to review menu options before ordering and help them make informed decisions based on availability, price, and expected service conditions.

This feature supports the Meal Ordering and Access epic because it reduces uncertainty before customers enter the ordering process. It also supports cafeteria staff by helping prevent customers from requesting items that are unavailable.

Order Placement and Order Summary

This feature allows customers to create an order by selecting available food and drink items. Before confirming the order, the system should present a clear order summary that includes selected items, quantities, and total cost.

This feature supports the Meal Ordering and Access epic by giving customers a structured way to submit orders and verify their choices before the order enters the cafeteria workflow.

Order and Payment Handling

This feature supports the creation and processing of customer orders, including the handling of payment confirmation and payment failure situations. It should ensure that customers receive clear feedback about whether their order and payment were completed successfully.

This feature connects to both the Meal Ordering and Access epic and the Payment Processing epic because payment status directly affects whether an order can move forward into preparation and pickup.

Order Status Tracking

This feature allows customers and cafeteria staff to track the progress of an order through its main service stages, such as submitted, being prepared, and ready for pickup.

This feature supports the Service Reliability and Coordination epic by reducing uncertainty and helping both customers and staff understand the current state of each order.

Pickup Notification Support

This feature notifies customers when their order is ready for pickup. The notification should be clear enough for the customer to know when to approach the pickup area without repeatedly asking staff for updates.

This feature supports the Meal Ordering and Access epic and the Service Reliability and Coordination epic by improving communication between the cafeteria and the customer.

Demand Awareness Support

This feature provides cafeteria staff with information about incoming orders and expected demand during service periods. It should help staff understand which items are being ordered, how many orders are waiting, and when demand is increasing.

This feature supports the Peak-Hour Flow Management epic because it gives staff better operational awareness during busy periods and helps them prepare meals under uncertain demand conditions.

Menu Availability Management

This feature allows cafeteria staff to update the availability of menu items when items run out or become temporarily unavailable. Customers should then see the updated availability before placing an order.

This feature supports both the Meal Ordering and Access epic and the Peak-Hour Flow Management epic by preventing unavailable items from creating delays, incorrect orders, or customer frustration.

Service Flow Coordination

This feature coordinates the progression from order placement to meal preparation and pickup. It should help ensure that orders move through the cafeteria workflow in an organized way and that customers receive accurate information about their order status.

This feature supports the Service Reliability and Coordination epic because it connects the customer-facing ordering experience with the staff-facing preparation process. It also supports peak-hour operations by helping reduce confusion when many orders are active at the same time.

2.2.3 Domain Requirements

The following requirements are derived from the domain description in Section 2.1. They define what the system must represent, enforce, or communicate in order to remain consistent with the cafeteria ordering domain.

  • DR-01: Order Composition

    • The system must associate every order with a non-empty set of menu items.

  • DR-02: Item Availability

    • The system must prevent a customer from adding an unavailable menu item to an order.

    • The system must track and expose whether each menu item is currently available for ordering.

  • DR-03: Order Ownership

    • The system must associate every placed order with the specific customer who initiated it.

  • DR-04: Order Preparation Lifecycle

    • The system must maintain a preparation status for each placed order and update that status when cafeteria staff record a change in the order’s preparation stage.

    • The system must make the current preparation status of each placed order available to cafeteria staff so that staff can identify which orders are pending, being prepared, or ready for pickup.

  • DR-05: Pricing and Total Calculation

    • The system must calculate the total price of an order based on the prices of the selected menu items.

  • DR-06: Order Fulfillment

    • The system must communicate to the customer when the customer’s specific order has transitioned to the ready-for-pickup state.

  • DR-07: Order Cancellation

    • The system must support the handling of order cancellations and notify cafeteria staff when an order has been canceled.

  • DR-08: Payment Confirmation Before Release

    • The system must represent the payment status of an order and must distinguish orders that are pending payment from those whose payment has been completed before the order is released to the customer.

  • DR-09: Customer Scope

    • The system must support order placement by cafeteria customers and must not assume that all customers are students.

  • DR-10: Preparation Time Variability

    • The system must represent preparation time as a variable property of an order rather than as a fixed constant for all orders.

  • DR-11: Pickup Code Assignment and Verification

    • The system must assign a unique pickup code to each placed order and make that code available to the ordering customer. The system must support cafeteria staff in verifying a customer-provided pickup code against the order’s recorded code before releasing the order.

2.2.4 Interface Requirements

Interface requirements describe how users interact with the Cafeteria Ordering System to enter, view, update, and verify information related to cafeteria operations. These requirements focus on the system boundary between users and the application interface, including how customers submit information and how authorized cafeteria staff manage operational information.

The terminology used in this section must match the terms defined in Section 2.1.2. This section does not introduce new terms or redefine existing ones. If a new term is needed, it must be added to the terminology section before being used in an interface requirement.

Interface Requirement Set

The requirements in this section use the convention IR-2.2.4-X.

The system shall provide an interface for authorized cafeteria staff to create the menu information shown in the application, including menu items, prices, categories, and item descriptions.

IR-2.2.4-2 Menu representation update

The system shall provide an interface for authorized cafeteria staff to update existing menu information when cafeteria offerings change, including changes to item price, item description, category, and availability.

IR-2.2.4-3 Availability synchronization

The system shall provide an interface for authorized cafeteria staff to mark menu items as available or unavailable so that the customer-facing menu reflects current cafeteria conditions.

The system shall update the customer-facing menu after an authorized staff member changes item availability, so customers do not attempt to order items that are no longer available.

IR-2.2.4-4 Customer menu visibility

The system shall provide an interface for customers to view the current menu, including item names, prices, categories, descriptions, and availability status.

The system shall visually distinguish unavailable menu items from available menu items in the customer interface.

IR-2.2.4-5 Order creation from domain observation

The system shall provide an interface for customers to create an order by selecting available menu items and specifying quantities.

The system shall prevent customers from selecting unavailable menu items during order creation.

IR-2.2.4-6 Order modification before submission

The system shall provide an interface for customers to review and modify an order before submission, including adding items, removing items, and changing item quantities.

The system shall display an updated order summary when a customer modifies an order before submission.

IR-2.2.4-7 Order submission and confirmation

The system shall provide an interface for customers to submit an order after reviewing the order summary.

After submission, the system shall display confirmation that the order has been recorded, including the order identifier, order summary, payment status, and pickup information when applicable.

IR-2.2.4-8 Payment method capture and constraints

The system shall provide an interface for customers to select an available payment method before submitting an order.

After an order is submitted, the system shall display the recorded payment status to the customer.

If payment cannot be completed or confirmed, the system shall display an interface message explaining that the order cannot proceed until the payment issue is resolved.

After an order is submitted, the system shall restrict changes to the recorded payment method unless the system provides an explicitly supported correction path, such as cancellation and resubmission.

IR-2.2.4-9 Order status synchronization (staff side)

The system shall provide an interface for authorized cafeteria staff to update the status of an order as it moves through the cafeteria workflow.

The staff interface shall support status updates such as submitted, being prepared, ready for pickup, picked up, and canceled, using only status terms defined in the terminology section.

IR-2.2.4-10 Order status visibility (customer side)

The system shall provide an interface for customers to view the current status of their submitted order.

The system shall update the customer-facing order status when authorized cafeteria staff change the order status through the staff interface.

IR-2.2.4-11 Correction after submission (controlled editing)

After an order is submitted, the system shall either prevent customer edits to the order contents or limit customer edits to explicitly supported actions, such as cancellation within an allowed window.

If staff-side corrections to a submitted order are supported, the system shall provide an interface for authorized cafeteria staff to record the correction and include a reason or note for the change.

IR-2.2.4-12 Immutable identifiers and record integrity

The system shall assign each submitted order a stable order identifier and display that identifier in the appropriate customer and staff interfaces.

The system shall prevent interface actions from changing core submitted order information, including the order identifier and submission timestamp, except through explicitly supported correction operations.

====== IR-2.2.4-13 Interface decomposition note

Some requirements that appear to be interface-only include both interface and non-interface aspects. For example, “the system supports creating an order” includes:

* An interface requirement aspect: the system provides UI mechanisms for customers to select items and submit an order.

* A non-interface aspect: the system creates and stores an internal order representation and enforces rules.

This section focuses on the boundary/interface aspects: how information is captured and kept consistent when the domain changes. Domain rules and internal constraints are specified in the domain and machine requirements sections.

IR-2.2.4-14 Pickup code generation and display

The system shall generate a pickup code for each submitted order and display the assigned pickup code to the customer after order confirmation.

The system shall provide an interface for cafeteria staff to enter or verify a customer-provided pickup code against the code recorded for the submitted order before releasing the order.

The system shall display whether the pickup code verification succeeds or fails so cafeteria staff can determine whether the order should be released.

====== IR-2.2.4-15 Staff order queue visibility

The system shall provide an interface for authorized cafeteria staff to view submitted orders in a staff-facing order queue.

The staff-facing order queue shall display enough information for cafeteria staff to identify the order, review selected items, view payment status, and determine the current order status.

====== IR-2.2.4-16 Interface feedback for invalid actions

The system shall provide clear interface feedback when a customer or cafeteria staff member attempts an invalid action, such as submitting an empty order, selecting an unavailable item, entering an incorrect pickup code, or attempting an unsupported order modification.

====== IR-2.2.4-17 Role-specific interface access

The system shall restrict staff-facing interface actions, including menu updates, availability updates, order status changes, and pickup code verification, to authorized cafeteria staff.

Order Creation and Validation

As said in the name, ordering is the core part of the Cafeteria Ordering App. Therefore, our system must validate a list of processes that ultimately validates the user’s order.

export async function createOrder(order: Order) {
  const { data, error } = await supabase.from('orders').insert({student_id: order.student_id, items: order.items, total: order.total, status: 'Pending',})

  if (error) {
    console.error('Error when creating order:', error.message)
    return null
  }

  await Notifications.scheduleNotificationAsync({
  content: {
    title: 'Order Confirmed',
    body: 'Your order has been successfully placed.',
  },
  trigger: null,
})

  return data
}

In this code, createOrder receives an Order object with the student ID, selected items, total price, and status, and saves it to the database. 4.1 TDD

This section demonstrates the application of Test-Driven Development (TDD) to incrementally construct a unit derived from the domain model.

Selected Unit: processPayment : PaymentSystem >< Order >< PaymentMethod → PaymentSystem >< (Option Receipt)

This operation corresponds to:

  • Action: Payment

  • Events: Payment Has Just Failed / Payment Has Just Been Processed Successfully

  • Behavior: Service Bottleneck caused by repeated failed payments

Initial Informal Specification

The function processes a customer’s payment attempt.

  • If the payment is approved, a receipt must be produced.

  • If the payment fails, no receipt is returned.

  • The system must record the outcome of the attempt.

Inputs: PaymentSystem, Order, PaymentMethod

Outputs: Updated PaymentSystem and Option Receipt.

Test 1 — A Successful Payment Produces a Result

A developer first checks that an approved payment produces some receipt.

Test: Given an approving payment gateway, calling processPayment should return Some(receipt).

Dummy Implementation:

return (ps, None)

Result: Test fails.

Minimal Implementation Change:

return (ps, Some(dummyReceipt))

Test now passes.

Test 2 — The Receipt Must Belong to the Correct Order

The first solution is too weak (it always returns a dummy receipt). The developer refines the behavior.

Test: The returned receipt must reference order.id.

Current implementation fails because it returns a constant receipt.

Implementation Update:

receipt = Receipt(order.id, order.total)
return (ps, Some(receipt))

Tests 1–2 now pass.

Test 3 — Failed Payments Return None

Next, failure behavior is introduced.

Test: If the gateway rejects the transaction, the function must return None.

Current implementation always succeeds → test fails.

Implementation Update:

if gateway.approve(order, method):
    receipt = Receipt(order.id, order.total)
    return (ps, Some(receipt))
else:
    return (ps, None)

Tests 1–3 now pass.

Test 4 — The System Must Record Successful Payments

To make payment outcomes observable, the system must log successes.

Test: After a successful payment, the updated PaymentSystem must contain a "SUCCESS" record for the order.

Current implementation does not modify system state → test fails.

Implementation Update:

if gateway.approve(order, method):
    ps2 = ps.addPaymentRecord(order.id, "SUCCESS")
    receipt = Receipt(order.id, order.total)
    return (ps2, Some(receipt))
else:
    return (ps, None)

Tests 1–4 now pass.

Test 5 — Failed Attempts Must Also Be Recorded

To support analysis of repeated failures (service bottlenecks), failed attempts must also be logged.

Test: If payment fails, PaymentSystem must record "FAILED".

Current implementation does not record failures → test fails.

Implementation Update:

if gateway.approve(order, method):
    ps2 = ps.addPaymentRecord(order.id, "SUCCESS")
    receipt = Receipt(order.id, order.total)
    return (ps2, Some(receipt))
else:
    ps2 = ps.addPaymentRecord(order.id, "FAILED")
    return (ps2, None)

All five tests now pass simultaneously.

Refactoring

With the behavior stabilized by tests, duplication is removed safely.

def recordAndReturn(ps, orderId, status, receiptOpt):
    ps2 = ps.addPaymentRecord(orderId, status)
    return (ps2, receiptOpt)

This refactoring does not change observable behavior. All tests remain passing, confirming correctness.

Result

Through five incremental tests:

  • The function evolved from a stub into meaningful behavior.

  • Each change was driven by a failing test.

  • Earlier tests continued to pass as new ones were added.

  • The final unit now reflects the domain:

    • successful payments generate receipts,

    • failures are observable events,

    • repeated failures can be analyzed as part of service bottlenecks.

This demonstrates the use of TDD to construct functionality through verifiable, incremental behavior.

4.5 Hoare Logic Testing

The project applies Hoare Logic to the Stripe payment workflow, one of the system’s core features. Because the project is implemented in TypeScript and React Native, for which no Hoare-logic verifier exists, the workflow was rewritten in Dafny to obtain tool-supported verification. Dafny encodes preconditions and postconditions directly as requires and ensures clauses and statically verifies them using the Z3 SMT solver. The full specification is maintained in documentation/LectureTopicTasks/Hoare Payment Workflow/hoare-payment-spec.adoc.

Focus Function: initiate_payment()

initiate_payment() is the entry point of the payment workflow. It transitions the system from a pre-payment state to an active payment state by marking the payment as initiated, setting the pending flag, and generating a payment identifier. Errors in this transition,such as initiating payment twice or generating no identifier, would corrupt every downstream step, making it an important function to verify formally.

The system state relevant to this function is:

order_exists      : Boolean
payment_initiated : Boolean
payment_pending   : Boolean
payment_id        : Integer  (0 = absent)

The Hoare triple for initiate_payment() is:

{ order_exists = true ∧ payment_initiated = false }
  initiate_payment()
{ payment_initiated = true ∧ payment_pending = true ∧ payment_id ≠ 0 }

The precondition enforces that an order exists and that payment has not already been started. The postcondition guarantees that all three state flags are correctly set after the call.

Proof of initiate_payment()

The body of initiate_payment() is a sequence of three assignments. Applying the assignment rule (A1) backwards from the postcondition through each statement derives the required precondition:

{ order_exists = true ∧ payment_initiated = false }
  ⟹ { true }                                           -- precondition implies true

  { true }                                              -- A1 on payment_id := 1
  payment_id := 1;

  { payment_initiated = true ∧ payment_pending = true } -- simplify: 1 ≠ 0 is true
  payment_pending := true;

  { payment_initiated = true }                          -- A1 on payment_pending := true
  payment_initiated := true;

{ payment_initiated = true ∧ payment_pending = true ∧ payment_id ≠ 0 }

Since the precondition implies true, the triple is totally correct: the function always terminates and always establishes the postcondition when called in a valid state.

Dafny Implementation and Verification

The function was encoded in Dafny as part of a PaymentWorkflow class covering the full workflow. The initiate_payment method and the class constructor are shown below:

class PaymentWorkflow {

  var order_exists: bool
  var payment_initiated: bool
  var payment_pending: bool
  var payment_success: bool
  var payment_failed: bool
  var order_confirmed: bool
  var order_cancelled: bool
  var payment_id: int
  var confirmation_id: int

  constructor()
    ensures order_exists == true
    ensures !payment_initiated && !payment_pending
    ensures !payment_success && !payment_failed
    ensures !order_confirmed && !order_cancelled
    ensures payment_id == 0 && confirmation_id == 0
  {
    order_exists      := true;
    payment_initiated := false;  payment_pending := false;
    payment_success   := false;  payment_failed  := false;
    order_confirmed   := false;  order_cancelled := false;
    payment_id        := 0;      confirmation_id := 0;
  }

  method initiate_payment()
    requires order_exists && !payment_initiated
    modifies this
    ensures payment_initiated && payment_pending && payment_id != 0
  {
    payment_initiated := true;
    payment_pending   := true;
    payment_id        := 1;
  }

  method confirm_payment()
    requires payment_initiated && payment_pending
    modifies this
    ensures payment_success && !payment_pending && !payment_failed
  {
    payment_success := true;
    payment_pending := false;
    payment_failed  := false;
  }

  method finalize_order()
    requires payment_success && !order_confirmed && !order_cancelled
    modifies this
    ensures order_confirmed && confirmation_id != 0
  {
    order_confirmed := true;
    confirmation_id := 1;
  }

  method cancel_order()
    requires (payment_failed || payment_pending) && !order_confirmed
    modifies this
    ensures order_cancelled && !order_confirmed && confirmation_id == 0
  {
    order_cancelled := true;
    confirmation_id := 0;
  }
}

Dafny’s verifier accepts the class without errors, confirming that every requires/ensures pair is satisfied by the implementation. The sequential composition of the happy path also holds:

{ order_exists = true }
  initiate_payment();
  confirm_payment();
  finalize_order();
{ order_confirmed = true }

4.6 Mutation Testing

The project uses Stryker (stryker-mutator.io) as its main testing tool to conduct mutation tests on all essential system elements. Stryker serves as the optimal testing solution for TypeScript and React Native systems because it provides direct integration with Jest and handles all essential mutation operators used in the codebase through its logical operator replacement and arithmetic operator replacement and constant boundary shift capabilities while generating per module mutation score reports. The complete mutation testing evaluation exists in four LTT documents that are stored in documentation/LectureTopicTasks/.

Component Prioritization

Before applying Stryker, the team defined a prioritization order for mutation testing based on three factors: criticality to system correctness, complexity of conditional logic, and the likelihood that weak assertions would allow mutants to survive. The resulting priority order is:

  1. Order Processing Logic — High priority. Contains conditional checks, boundary conditions, and decision-making rules that directly determine whether user orders are accepted or rejected.

  2. Pricing and Calculation Logic — High priority. Arithmetic mutations (e.g., replacing + with -, shifting constants by ±1) produce incorrect totals that can pass undetected if tests only verify that a value is returned rather than verifying its exact amount.

  3. Ingredient Handling — Medium priority. Simpler logic, but combination-rule mutations and validation-guard removals can still expose test gaps.

  4. Menu Management — Low priority. Relatively static logic; mutation testing provides the least incremental value here compared to higher-complexity components.

4.10 Fuzzing

Fuzzing can be applied to the Cafeteria Ordering System to test how the web interface responds to unexpected, malformed, or invalid inputs. Unlike regular example-based testing, which checks a small number of predefined cases, fuzzing generates many unusual input values to discover weaknesses in input validation, error handling, and interface behavior.

This is useful for the Cafeteria Ordering System because users interact with forms and interface elements such as order creation, item quantity selection, payment method selection, special instructions, pickup information, and login or profile fields. These inputs should remain stable and predictable even when users submit unexpected values.

Fuzzing Scope

For this project, the fuzzing strategy focuses on the web interface and order-related input flow. The main goal is to verify that invalid or unexpected inputs do not crash the interface, create invalid orders, bypass validation rules, or produce unclear feedback for the user.

The main interface areas that can benefit from fuzzing include:

  • Order item quantity fields

  • Special instruction text fields

  • Payment-related input fields

  • Menu item selection controls

  • Pickup code or order verification fields

  • Login, sign-up, and profile text fields

The fuzzing process can be organized around the order submission flow:

  1. Generate unexpected values for order-related fields.

  4. Verify that invalid inputs do not create valid orders.

  6. Record any crash, unclear message, layout issue, or invalid accepted state as a defect.

4.9 Types

The Cafeteria Ordering System incorporates static typing through TypeScript to make core domain concepts easier to understand, validate, and maintain. Since the project uses TypeScript, domain concepts such as orders, menu items, payments, and order statuses can be represented with explicit types instead of loosely structured objects.

Static typing is useful in this project because it clarifies what kind of data each function expects and returns. This reduces ambiguity when developers work with order creation, menu item selection, payment processing, and order status updates.

Core Domain Types

The following TypeScript example represents important cafeteria ordering concepts using meaningful static types:

type MenuItemId = string;
type OrderId = string;

type PaymentMethod = "cashier" | "card";
type OrderStatus = "draft" | "pending_payment" | "confirmed" | "ready_for_pickup" | "cancelled";

type MenuItem = {
  id: MenuItemId;
  name: string;
  price: number;
  isAvailable: boolean;
};

type OrderItem = {
  menuItemId: string;
  quantity: number;
};

type Order = {
  items: OrderItem[];
  paymentMethod: "cashier" | "card";
};

function validateOrder(order: Order, menuItems: MenuItem[]): boolean {
  if (order.items.length === 0) {
    return false;
  }

  return order.items.every((item) => {
    const menuItem = menuItems.find((menuItem) => menuItem.id === item.menuItemId);

    return (
      menuItem !== undefined &&
      menuItem.isAvailable &&
      item.quantity > 0 &&
      item.quantity <= 10
    );
  });
}

Meaningful Properties

The following properties describe general rules that should hold for the cafeteria ordering flow.

Property 1 — Order Total Should Never Be Negative

For any valid generated order, the calculated total should always be greater than or equal to zero. Since menu item prices and item quantities determine the order total, the system should reject or prevent invalid values that could produce a negative total.

property("order total is never negative", (generatedOrder) => {
  const total = calculateOrderTotal(generatedOrder);
  expect(total).toBeGreaterThanOrEqual(0);
});

This property helps detect invalid price or quantity handling, such as negative prices, negative quantities, or incorrect total calculations.

Property 2 — A Valid Order Must Contain at Least One Item

For any order to be valid, it must include at least one item. An empty order should not be accepted because it does not represent a real cafeteria request.

property("valid orders contain at least one item", (generatedOrder) => {
  if (validateOrder(generatedOrder, menuItems)) {
    expect(generatedOrder.items.length).toBeGreaterThan(0);
  }
});

This property supports the cafeteria ordering domain because every submitted order should represent one or more requested menu items.

Property 3 — Item Quantities Must Stay Within Valid Limits

Generated orders should only be valid when each item quantity is within the accepted range. For example, a quantity of zero, a negative quantity, or an unreasonably large quantity should cause the order to be rejected.

property("item quantities remain within valid limits", (generatedOrder) => {
  const isValid = validateOrder(generatedOrder, menuItems);

  if (isValid) {
    generatedOrder.items.forEach((item) => {
      expect(item.quantity).toBeGreaterThan(0);
      expect(item.quantity).toBeLessThanOrEqual(10);
    });
  }
});

This property helps verify that the system does not accept unrealistic or invalid order quantities.

Property 4 — Invalid Menu Item IDs Should Not Create Valid Orders

If a generated order contains a menu item ID that does not exist in the menu catalog, the order should not be accepted as valid.

property("invalid menu item ids do not create valid orders", (generatedOrder) => {
  const containsInvalidItem = generatedOrder.items.some((item) => {
    return !menuItems.some((menuItem) => menuItem.id === item.menuItemId);
  });

  if (containsInvalidItem) {
    expect(validateOrder(generatedOrder, menuItems)).toBe(false);
  }
});

This property is important because the system should not allow users to submit orders for items that are not part of the cafeteria menu.

Generated Test Cases and Edge Cases

Generated test cases help reveal edge cases that developers may not manually consider. For example, a property-based testing tool could generate empty orders, orders with repeated items, invalid menu item identifiers, unavailable menu items, very large quantities, or unexpected combinations of valid and invalid items.

This makes the testing process stronger because the same system rule is checked across many input combinations. If one generated case breaks a property, the test can expose a weakness in validation logic that might not be found through a small set of example-based tests. ==== Functional Insight

The calculateOrderTotal function receives an Order and returns a number. This makes the function signature clear because developers can immediately understand that the function works with a complete order object and produces a numeric total.

The type definitions also prevent common mistakes. For example, paymentMethod can only be "cashier" or "card", and status can only use one of the defined order lifecycle values. This prevents unclear values such as "paid later" or "done" from being used when the system expects a specific domain value.

Relevance to the Project

Fuzzing strengthens the reliability of the Cafeteria Ordering System by testing the interface against inputs that real users may accidentally or intentionally submit. Since the system handles important flows such as order creation, payment selection, and pickup verification, it must reject invalid data safely and consistently.

By documenting a fuzzing strategy for the web interface, the project shows how generated unexpected inputs can help reveal validation weaknesses, improve robustness, and reduce the risk of invalid cafeteria orders being processed. Property-based testing supports the Cafeteria Ordering System by validating general ordering rules at a higher level. These rules are connected to the web API and order validation flow because the system must determine whether submitted order data is valid before processing payment, notifying staff, or adding the order to the active queue.

By documenting these properties, the project shows how property-based testing can be used to strengthen confidence in order validation and reduce the risk of accepting invalid cafeteria orders. These types improve the clarity of the Cafeteria Ordering System by making the domain model explicit. Instead of treating orders, menu items, and payments as generic objects, the system can represent them as meaningful concepts with defined structure and expected values.

This supports maintainability because future developers can understand the shape of the data before modifying order creation, payment handling, or pickup-related functionality. It also supports reliability because invalid values are more likely to be detected during development before they cause runtime errors.
